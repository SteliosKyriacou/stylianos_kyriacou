On the use of Multi-Objective Optimization algorithms powered by Alphafold-2 and Structure-to-activity models for Protein design

With the recent availability of accurate sequence to structure prediction models (Alphafold 2) and the availability of a number of both physics and ML driven structure to activity of interest models, protein design problems can, now, be addressed at their complete scale and complexity. Removing previously necessary adhoc simplifications protein design problems can now be addressed as complete Constraint Multi-Objective Optimization (CMOO) problems.

Solving protein design as a CMOO problem is near impossible for humans and extremely difficult for algorithms due to a number of reasons. The design space (the number of possible sequences) is near infinite and therefore exhaustive search is not feasible. There are multiple, contradictory, objectives (activity vs selectivity if an enzyme is sought after) hence common, single objective, search algorithms are ill suited. Instead powerful multi-objective optimizations algorithms (seeking the Pareto front of non dominated solutions) are required. The complete problem formulation typically contains hard constraints (such as stability). The objective function/s landscape (mapping between sequence and activity metrics) is complex. It is not globally convex, and therefore algorithms that rely on convexity will fail, and is multi-modal (it contains multiple local optima) therefore algorithms that perform better at global optimization tasks are required. In addition to the above evaluation of the objective function/s can be computationally demanding (~30 minutes per sequence on a K80 GPU) which means that wall-clock time efficient algorithms are required if solutions are to be discovered in acceptable by industry times (days not months or years). For the aforementioned reasons, a powerful Cloud Distributed Metamodel-Assisted optimization Algorithm was chosen for this task.

Enzyme design case

An enzyme design problem is used to demonstrate the aforementioned methods capabilities. In this task both the activity and enantioselectivity of an enzyme are sought after. This is a particularly important task in the field of pharmaceuticals, as different enantiomers of a molecule often demonstrate different biological activity.

The multi-objective optimization problem addressing this task is defined as; locate the sequence/s that:

  • maximize activity against the (+) enantiomer
  • minimize activity against the (-) enantiomer

A cluster of 100 GPUs and 1600 CPUs was used to search the enzyme sequence space using the algorithm depicted in Fig 1 in order to solve the aforementioned problem. During this task tenths of thousands potential enzymes were evaluated over a period of 24 hours. The results of this approach are summarized in fig 2. With black the wild type (WT) enzyme is depicted, notice that the WT is active against both enantiomers (and therefore not selective). With red are the members of the Pareto front, these enzymes correspond to the optimal compromises between the 2 objectives. The far right one maximizes activity over the (+) enantiomer (~10% better than the WT) but also shows some undesirable activity against the (-) enantiomer. The far left one has the same activity against the (+) enantiomer as the WT and almost zero activity against the (-) enantiomer (hence optimizes selectivity).

A comparison between the Wild type enzyme and a Pareto member enzyme can be seen in fig 3. In accordance with the objective of the optimization problem, the Pareto member enzyme, thru a number of mutations has altered its structure so as to keep the (+) enantiomer (cyan molecule) close to the catalytic triad (yellow spheres) while pushing the (-) enantiomer (magenta molecule) away from them. Hence, the Pareto member is predicted to have enhanced enantioselectivity in comparison to the Wild Type one.

A negative example, an enzyme predicted to have no activity against both enantiomers, is shown in Fig 4. A switch of a slimmer residue with a bulgier one at the vicinity of the binding pocket reduces its volume to such an extent that binding of either substrate there is highly improbable
