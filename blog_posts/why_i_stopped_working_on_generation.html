Why I Stopped Working on Molecular Generation

Author: Stylianos Kyriacou

Post 1 of 9 on SAISHO’s clinical-outcomes prediction model.

I spent a big part of my career building systems that produce molecules. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for de novo design at Norachem. Multi-objective optimizers driven by AlphaFold-2 embeddings. Enzyme engineering at industrial scale at Aether. The through line was always the same: treat design as a search problem, then make the search cheap enough to run at scale.

For practical purposes that problem is now solved, and you can see it in where the capital and the attention have gone. Isomorphic Labs raised $2.1 billion in May on the premise that accurate structure prediction makes design tractable. Chai Discovery has put frontier structure and interaction models within reach of anyone who wants them, and Lilly is deploying them across its portfolio. Anthropic now runs an internal wet lab, and when it launched I wrote that generating candidate molecules in two hours was a genuine milestone for computational chemistry. It was.

Generative chemistry and automated synthesis have collapsed the cost of proposing a plausible candidate to something close to zero. Ten thousand molecules satisfying every heuristic you care to name, generated before lunch.

Which is exactly why I stopped working on it.

The Question That Costs Two Billion Dollars to Answer

There is one question in drug development that the industry still answers by running an eye-wateringly expensive experiment, one that also carries real risk to the patients enrolled in it: will this molecule, against this target, in this indication, become a therapy?

The experiment is a clinical trial. It takes years, it costs between tens of millions and a couple of billion dollars depending on how far you get before the answer arrives, and roughly nine times out of ten the answer is no.

Proposing the candidate is close to free. Answering that question is not, and no amount of additional generation makes it cheaper. Faster generation without better selection does not fix attrition, it scales it: more candidates entering the same funnel, with the same nine-in-ten failure rate applied to a larger number. The binding constraint moved from what can we design to what should we advance, and those are not the same problem with the same solution.

So we stopped asking how to make more molecules and started asking how to tell, early, which molecule-target-indication combinations survive contact with a patient population. That is what I have spent the last two years on.

What I Help Build

SAISHO is our answer. Architecturally it behaves like a drug review board rather than a classifier, and that distinction carries most of the performance, for reasons I will spend post 3 on.

Four domain specialists (medicinal chemistry, target biology, pharmacology, toxicology) evaluate the same program independently and in parallel. None of them sees the others’ work while forming a view. That is a deliberate constraint rather than an implementation detail: it removes the anchoring you get when one confident opinion arrives before the rest. A reconciliation pass then ingests all four advisories, resolves the contradictions between them, and emits a single Clinical Risk Score on a 0 to 100 scale (higher is riskier) together with a ranked list of the specific liabilities driving it.

The inputs are deliberately minimal: a structure as SMILES, the biological target or mechanism of action, and the therapeutic indication. Optional extras (route of administration, dosing regimen, emerging preclinical data) sharpen the assessment, but those three are sufficient to run. That constraint is the entire point. All three exist during lead optimization, candidate selection and indication positioning, which is precisely the window in which the decisions that matter are still reversible. A model that needs Phase 2 data to make a call is answering a question nobody is still asking.

What We Found

  • On a benchmark of 391 small-molecule programs with confirmed outcomes, under leakage controls that stripped compound identities and disabled similarity search, the Clinical Risk Score separates approved from failed programs with an AUROC of 0.90 and an average precision of 0.89.
  • Across the failed or withdrawn programs in that cohort, the reconciled assessment prioritized a liability matching the documented reason for discontinuation 77% of the time. Crediting a match found by any of the four specialists, rather than only the final reconciled view, raises it to 83%.
  • Stripped down to chemistry alone, average precision falls from roughly 0.90 to roughly 0.72. A frontier model asked the same question directly, in a single pass, with the same inputs, returns 0.56 to 0.61. The full workflow beats the strongest single-pass baseline by roughly 29 points of average precision.
  • Hold the molecule fixed and change only the indication or the delivery route, and the score moves, sometimes enormously. Valacyclovir scores 1 in herpes zoster and 88 in early symptomatic Alzheimer’s disease.
  • The two results I actually care about are prospective. On 83 programs whose outcomes were disclosed after the system’s January 2025 information boundary, average precision was 0.92. On a separate, non-overlapping set of 15 programs where the prediction was archived with a verifiable timestamp before the outcome was public, it was 0.87.

One of those numbers is genuinely small, and I will flag it every time it appears. The timestamped cohort has 15 resolved programs so far, with 193 more registered and still running. It is simultaneously the strongest evidence we have and the thinnest, and both halves of that sentence are worth saying out loud.
